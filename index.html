String of Pearls

Curio rowleyanus has near-spherical, pea-like leaves on fine trailing stems. The older label Senecio rowleyanus is still common.

Water by checking the mix

For String of Pearls and similar dry-tolerant succulents, let a free-draining mix dry before watering again. Soft or translucent leaves plus persistently wet mix suggest rot—not thirst.

Look at leaf shape first

Hearts, bananas, dolphins, coins, needles and stacked buttons point to different plants. Common names overlap, so use foliage and a botanical label together.

Fast checks for String of Pearls

Read the symptom with the soil

  • Wrinkled but still firm, green pearls + dry mix: dehydration is plausible; water thoroughly and let it drain.
  • Soft, translucent, dark pearls or stems + wet mix: suspect rot; stop routine watering and inspect roots and stems.
  • Long gaps between pearls / weak stems: check light and consider gradual pruning.
  • White cottony clusters: isolate the plant and inspect for mealybugs.

Australian position and seasonal notes

  • Humid coastal weather, rain exposure, a large pot and low airflow can slow drying.
  • Small pots and dry inland heat can shorten the interval—but heat alone is not a reason to water damp mix.
  • Reduce watering when growth slows in cooler weather and protect the plant from frost and prolonged cold.
  • Keep String of Pearls away from children and pets that may chew it; ingestion can be harmful and sap may irritate skin.
Safety reminder: Do not treat this table as a complete pet-toxicity register. Confirm the exact species and seek veterinary or poisons advice if ingestion is suspected.

How to use this reference

Common names are not standardised, especially for “String of Tears”, “String of Watermelons” and “String of Dolphins”. When a plant’s appearance does not match its retail name, compare its foliage and confirm the botanical label before changing its care routine. The care notes are intentionally broad and should be adjusted to the pot, mix, season and local conditions.
